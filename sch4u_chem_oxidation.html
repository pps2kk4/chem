Part 1 · 3 min

Oxidation Numbers

An oxidation number (ON) is a formal value used to track electron movement during a reaction.

Oxidation

ON increases ↑

The species loses electrons.

Reduction

ON decreases ↓

The species gains electrons.

OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss; Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).
Part 1 · 3 min

Oxidation-Number Rules

  • An uncombined element has ON = 0 (e.g., I₂, P₄, Fe).
  • A monatomic ion’s ON equals its charge (e.g., Fe²⁺ = +2).
  • Oxygen is generally −2 (except in peroxides where it is −1).
  • Hydrogen is generally +1 when bonded to nonmetals.
  • The sum of oxidation numbers equals the net charge.
Neutral compound: sum = 0
Ion: sum = ion charge
Part 1 · 2 min

Calculation Example: MnO₄⁻

Let Mn = x. Each oxygen is −2. The net charge of the ion is −1.

x + 4(−2) = −1
x − 8 = −1
x = +7
MnO₄⁻ is a polyatomic ion with an overall charge of −1. Therefore, the algebraic sum of the individual oxidation numbers must equal −1, not 0.

Part 2 · 3 min

The Oxidation-Number Method

  1. Assign oxidation numbers to all atoms.
  2. Identify which ONs increase and decrease.
  3. Calculate the total electron change per molecule/ion.
  4. Determine coefficients to equalize electrons lost and gained.
  5. Balance remaining atoms (O with H₂O, H with H⁺).
  6. Convert to basic conditions if required.
Multiply the ON change per atom by the number of those atoms present in the formula.
Part 2 · 3 min

Equalizing Electron Transfer

Assume atom A's ON increases by 3 (loses 3 e⁻) and atom B's ON decreases by 2 (gains 2 e⁻).

2 × 3 = 6 electrons lost
3 × 2 = 6 electrons gained

The lowest common multiple is 6. You require 2 atoms of A for every 3 atoms of B to conserve charge.

Part 3 · 2 min

Acidic Solution Procedure

Balance the following reaction in an acidic solution:

MnO₄⁻ + CH₃OH → MnO₂ + CO₂

First, identify the oxidation numbers that change.

Mn: +7 in MnO₄⁻ → +4 in MnO₂ (Reduced, gains 3 e⁻).
C: −2 in CH₃OH → +4 in CO₂ (Oxidized, loses 6 e⁻).
Part 3 · 3 min

Step 1: Set the Redox Coefficients

Manganese

+7 → +4

Gains 3 e⁻ per Mn.

Carbon

−2 → +4

Loses 6 e⁻ per C.

To equalize the electron transfer (6 e⁻), two Mn atoms are required for every one C atom.

2MnO₄⁻ + 1CH₃OH → 2MnO₂ + 1CO₂
Part 3 · 3 min

Step 2: Balance Oxygen

2MnO₄⁻ + CH₃OH → 2MnO₂ + CO₂
SideOxygen Count
Left8 (from 2MnO₄⁻) + 1 (from CH₃OH) = 9
Right4 (from 2MnO₂) + 2 (from CO₂) = 6

The right side is deficient by 3 oxygen atoms. Add 3H₂O to the right side.

2MnO₄⁻ + CH₃OH → 2MnO₂ + CO₂ + 3H₂O
Part 3 · 3 min

Step 3: Balance Hydrogen

2MnO₄⁻ + CH₃OH → 2MnO₂ + CO₂ + 3H₂O

Count the hydrogen atoms:

  • Left side: 4 H (from CH₃OH)
  • Right side: 6 H (from 3H₂O)

The left side requires 2 more hydrogen atoms. Add 2H⁺ to the left side.

2MnO₄⁻ + CH₃OH + 2H⁺ → 2MnO₂ + CO₂ + 3H₂O
Part 3 · 2 min

Acidic Solution: Final Verification

2MnO₄⁻ + CH₃OH + 2H⁺ → 2MnO₂ + CO₂ + 3H₂O
MetricLeft SideRight Side
Mn Atoms22
C Atoms11
Charge2(−1) + 2(+1) = 00
The atom counts are equal and the total charge is balanced. The procedure is complete.
Part 4 · 2 min

Basic Solution Procedure

For basic solutions, balance the reaction as if it were acidic first.

Then, perform the following conversion:

  1. Add OH⁻ to both sides equal to the number of H⁺ ions.
  2. Combine H⁺ and OH⁻ on the same side to form H₂O.
  3. Cancel out redundant H₂O molecules that appear on both sides.
H⁺ ions cannot remain in the final equation for a basic solution.
Part 4 · 3 min

Basic Solution: Step 1

Balance the following in a basic solution:

I⁻ + MnO₄⁻ → I₂ + MnO₂

Determine the electron transfer:

  • I: −1 → 0. Two I⁻ are required to make I₂, losing a total of 2 e⁻.
  • Mn: +7 → +4. Gains 3 e⁻.

LCM of 2 and 3 is 6. We require 3 molecules of I₂ (6 e⁻ lost) and 2 of Mn (6 e⁻ gained).

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ → 3I₂ + 2MnO₂
Part 4 · 3 min

Basic Solution: Step 2

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ → 3I₂ + 2MnO₂

Balance O and H as if the solution were acidic:

Left side has 8 O. Right side has 4 O. Add 4H₂O to the right.

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ → 3I₂ + 2MnO₂ + 4H₂O

Right side has 8 H. Left side has 0 H. Add 8H⁺ to the left.

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 3I₂ + 2MnO₂ + 4H₂O
Part 4 · 4 min

Basic Solution: Conversion

Currently: 6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 3I₂ + 2MnO₂ + 4H₂O

Add 8OH⁻ to both sides to neutralize the H⁺.

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ + (8H⁺ + 8OH⁻)
→ 3I₂ + 2MnO₂ + 4H₂O + 8OH⁻

Combine H⁺ and OH⁻ into water.

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ + 8H₂O
→ 3I₂ + 2MnO₂ + 4H₂O + 8OH⁻

Cancel 4H₂O from both sides.

6I⁻ + 2MnO₄⁻ + 4H₂O
→ 3I₂ + 2MnO₂ + 8OH⁻
Part 5 · 3 min

Disproportionation Reactions

In a disproportionation reaction, a single reactant acts as both the oxidizing and reducing agent.

P₄ → PH₃ + H₂PO₂⁻ (Basic solution)

Assign oxidation numbers to Phosphorus:

  • In P₄: ON = 0
  • In PH₃: ON = −3 (Gains 3 e⁻ per P)
  • In H₂PO₂⁻: ON = +1 (Loses 1 e⁻ per P)

Notice the ratio: 1 P atom is reduced for every 3 P atoms that are oxidized.

Part 5 · 3 min

Disproportionation: Redox Ratio

Ratio: 1 P atom (gains 3 e⁻) : 3 P atoms (lose 1 e⁻ each).

This equals exactly 4 P atoms, which corresponds to one molecule of P₄.

Therefore, one P₄ molecule produces one PH₃ and three H₂PO₂⁻.

1P₄ → 1PH₃ + 3H₂PO₂⁻

Next, balance the O and H as if in acidic solution.

Right side has 6 O. Add 6H₂O to left.
P₄ + 6H₂O → PH₃ + 3H₂PO₂⁻
Left side has 12 H. Right side has 3 + 6 = 9 H. Add 3H⁺ to the right.
P₄ + 6H₂O → PH₃ + 3H₂PO₂⁻ + 3H⁺
Part 5 · 2 min

Disproportionation: Convert to Basic

Acidic state: P₄ + 6H₂O → PH₃ + 3H₂PO₂⁻ + 3H⁺

Add 3OH⁻ to both sides.

P₄ + 6H₂O + 3OH⁻ → PH₃ + 3H₂PO₂⁻ + 3H₂O

Simplify the H₂O.

P₄ + 3H₂O + 3OH⁻ → PH₃ + 3H₂PO₂⁻
Check charge: Left = −3. Right = 3(−1) = −3. Balanced.
Review · 2 min

Procedural Errors to Avoid

  • Failing to account for subscripts: When calculating total electron transfer, the ON change must be multiplied by the number of atoms undergoing the change in the formula.
  • Balancing O and H prematurely: The primary redox coefficients must be locked in before adding water or hydrogen ions.
  • Incomplete basic conversion: Leaving H⁺ in the final equation or forgetting to add OH⁻ to the side opposite the H⁺.
  • Skipping the charge check: The most reliable way to catch an error is verifying that the sum of charges on the left equals the sum on the right.

Practice · 4 min

Practice A — Identifying Roles

Consider the reaction:

Fe²⁺ + Ce⁴⁺ → Fe³⁺ + Ce³⁺

Identify the species oxidized, the species reduced, the oxidizing agent, and the reducing agent.

Oxidized: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ (loses an electron, ON increases).
Reduced: Ce⁴⁺ → Ce³⁺ (gains an electron, ON decreases).
Oxidizing agent: Ce⁴⁺ (it causes oxidation by undergoing reduction).
Reducing agent: Fe²⁺ (it causes reduction by undergoing oxidation).
Practice · 5 min

Practice B — Acidic Solution

Balance the following in acidic solution:

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Cr³⁺ + Fe³⁺

Guidance: Cr changes from +6 to +3. Notice there are 2 Cr atoms in the dichromate ion. Fe changes from +2 to +3.

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O + 6Fe³⁺
Explanation: Two Cr atoms gain a total of 6 electrons. Therefore, 6 Fe²⁺ ions are required to provide 6 electrons.
Practice · 5 min

Practice C — Basic Solution

Balance this half-reaction in a basic solution:

Cl⁻ → ClO₃⁻

Balance O with H₂O, H with H⁺, convert using OH⁻, and add electrons to balance the charge.

Cl⁻ + 6OH⁻ → ClO₃⁻ + 3H₂O + 6e⁻
Explanation: Cl changes from −1 to +5, an oxidation producing 6e⁻ on the product side. Charge on left: −7. Charge on right: −1 + −6 = −7.
Summary · 2 min

Procedure Checklist

  1. Assigned oxidation numbers to all elements.
  2. Identified ON changes and total electron transfer per formula unit.
  3. Determined lowest common multiple to set the primary redox coefficients.
  4. Balanced O with H₂O and H with H⁺.
  5. If basic, neutralized H⁺ by adding OH⁻ to both sides and simplifying H₂O.
  6. Verified that both atom count and total charge are equal on both sides.
If the final check fails, locate the step where the imbalance occurred rather than arbitrarily adjusting coefficients.
